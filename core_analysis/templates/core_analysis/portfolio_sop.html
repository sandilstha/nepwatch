Standard Operating Procedure

This document is the single source of truth for how every number on the Risk & Portfolio desk is computed: the formula, the inputs, the data source, the update frequency, the assumptions behind it, and how to read it. Every (?) icon on the dashboard links to the matching section here.

Section 1

Purpose & scope

The desk answers five questions about your private NEPSE account: What is it worth? (valuation), Where is it exposed? (sector, concentration), How much can it lose? (volatility, VaR, drawdown, stress) and How fast can I get out? (liquidity), plus What has been booked? (broker cash, transactions and realised P/L).

Because the Meroshare “My Shares” export carries only balances and prices, the desk is position-risk first. Cost-based measures (Book Value, Paper P/L) appear only after you import your broker's “My WACC” report. The optional Account Statement adds transaction accounting and broker cash without replacing either snapshot. Nothing here is investment advice; all figures are estimates on end-of-day data.

Section 3

Imports: Holdings, WACC & Broker Ledger

3.0 Managing multiple portfolios

Open Manage portfolios, then expand Create new portfolio to add a new account using the ordered inputs: Name, Upload Holdings, Upload WACC, Create Portfolio. Name may be left blank when the holdings report contains Holder Name or BO Name; WACC is optional. Both files are validated before the portfolio and its rows are created in one database transaction. Portfolio names are unique per account without case sensitivity. For an already-created active portfolio, Manage portfolios → Active portfolio actions exposes only Delete Portfolio; it does not ask for duplicate holdings or WACC uploads and does not show rename, duplicate or archive actions in the day-to-day view. Deletion permanently removes that portfolio and its holdings, WACC and private ledger rows; the last active portfolio cannot be deleted. Archived portfolios may be restored, duplicated, renamed or permanently deleted from Manage portfolios.

3.1 Upload Holdings — “My Shares” file (CSV, Excel or PDF)

Export “My Shares” from Meroshare and upload it from the Create new portfolio panel. New-portfolio imports run when Create Portfolio is submitted. When the report includes Holder Name or BO Name, that value becomes the portfolio name automatically; otherwise the selected portfolio type provides the fallback name. The parser locates the Scrip, Current Balance, Last Closing Price and LTP columns by header name (order-independent), skips header/total/blank lines and zero balances, and de-duplicates symbols. PDF uploads are read table-by-table across every page; Excel workbooks use the active sheet. The created portfolio receives those holdings, while cost basis (WACC) is stored separately.

3.2 Import WACC — broker “My WACC” report (CSV, Excel or PDF)

Adds your weighted-average cost per scrip. Rows are matched to holdings by symbol; unmatched scrips are kept (they re-match on the next holdings upload). This unlocks the Overview's Investment and Unrealised Gain/Loss values plus the Expected Unrealised P/L scenario tile.

3.3 Broker Ledger & Nepali fiscal years

Open Manage portfolios → Broker ledger & Nepali FY, choose the broker Account Statement, and select Import Ledger. The importer keeps the statement's AD date, BS date, voucher number, reference, branch, particulars, debit, credit and signed running balance. It recognizes opening balances, deposits/receipts, purchase bills, sale bills, payments/withdrawals, dividends, explicit charges, taxes, adjustments and unknown rows. Buy/sell text such as NABIL 100 kitta @ 500 becomes a separate trade leg for position and cost accounting.

signed broker cash: CR balance = positive; DR balance = negative expected closing balance = prior signed balance + credit − debit buy net cost = Σ(quantity × price) + bill deductions sell net proceeds = Σ(quantity × price) − bill deductions realised P/L = net sale proceeds − weighted-average cost of quantity sold Total Equity = current holdings market value + latest signed broker cash Total P/L = all-time realised P/L + current WACC-based unrealised P/L

Fiscal year. When BS is present, FY is exact: Shrawan 1 (BS month 04) starts the year, so 2082-04-01 belongs to FY 2082/83 while 2082-03-30 belongs to FY 2081/82. Only when BS is absent does the parser use an approximate July 16 AD boundary. The FY selector in that same manager panel reports opening/closing broker cash, purchase bills, sale bills, deposits, payments, dividends, deductions/taxes, realised P/L and the realised FY result. The FY transaction table is open by default, shows five transactions per page, and can be hidden or shown from its header without changing the selected year. The Holdings/WACC reconciliation issue list has the same hide/show control. On portfolios with holdings, it is integrated into the Concentration & Attribution card so position, cost-basis and concentration exceptions can be reviewed together. FY transactions remain in the Broker Ledger section below; an empty portfolio keeps reconciliation there as a fallback because no concentration card is displayed.

Duplicate and missing-row controls. The file is SHA-256 checked for exact re-imports. Each row also receives a content fingerprint, so overlapping statements merge without duplicating vouchers. Source serial-number gaps and running-balance breaks are flagged. Holdings reconciliation compares cumulative ledger trade quantity with the latest My Shares snapshot; WACC reconciliation compares ledger-derived remaining average cost where the quantity path is complete. A newer Holdings/WACC snapshot than the ledger is explicitly marked stale rather than treated as reconciled.

Cost-basis coverage. Realised P/L is confirmed when earlier ledger buys supply the sold quantity. If an opening lot is absent but a WACC row exists, the missing portion uses that WACC as a visibly labelled estimate. Without either source, the quantity and its P/L remain unresolved. The Account Statement exposes only the difference between trade gross and bill total, so commission, SEBON fee, DP charge and CGT remain a combined Charges & Taxes amount unless the statement provides separate rows.

Section 4 · Core methodology

Beta — weekly-return methodology

Purpose. Beta measures how much a holding (or the whole book) moves when the NEPSE Index moves 1%. β = 1 tracks the market; β > 1 amplifies it; β < 1 dampens it.

Betas on this desk are estimated on WEEKLY returns, not daily. This is deliberate and matches the structure of the Nepali capital market: many NEPSE scrips trade thinly and non-synchronously, so a stock's daily close often lags the index move by a session or more. Daily-return betas are therefore biased toward zero (the regression sees "index moved, stock didn't"). Sampling the last traded close of each Sun–Fri week lets laggards catch up inside the sampling interval, giving a materially less biased estimate.

Calculation

weekly return rᵢ,w = (Pᵢ,w − Pᵢ,w₋₁) / Pᵢ,w₋₁ P = last close of ISO week w stock beta βᵢ = Cov(rᵢ, r_m) / Var(r_m) OLS over shared weeks, ~52-week window portfolio beta β_p = Σ wᵢ · βᵢ weights re-based over the names that have a beta
Return frequencyWeekly (ISO week, Sun–Fri)
BenchmarkNEPSE Index (close)
Window370 days ≈ 52 weeks
Minimum sample26 shared weekly returns

Coverage control. Every holding row shows its individual beta. The portfolio KPI also reports the percentage of market value covered by valid betas. Covered weights are re-based to 100% for β_p; uncovered names are not silently assigned β = 1.

Assumptions & caveats

  • A week with no trade contributes no observation; the next observed week's return spans the gap (matching how thin names actually reprice).
  • Names with under ~6 months of history show “—” and are excluded from the portfolio beta (remaining weights are re-based so the estimate isn't diluted).
  • Circuit breakers truncate extreme weeks, so betas of limit-prone names are still conservative estimates.

Interpretation. β_p = 1.20 means a −10% NEPSE week is expected to cost the book ≈ −12%, before stock-specific news. Beta explains the market-driven part only — see the factor model for how much of your risk that actually is.

Section 3.9 · Core methodology

How reliable are these numbers?

Purpose. Every figure on this desk is computed to two decimals, but they are not all equally trustworthy. Some are arithmetic on today's positions; others are statistical estimates drawn from thin, illiquid price history. This section grades each metric group by the quality of the data feeding it, and the desk shows that grading at the top of the risk section.

Grades describe the INPUTS, not the risk. A red grade means "do not read this number to two decimals" — not "this holding is dangerous".

GradeMeans
ReliableArithmetic, or an estimate from essentially complete data
EstimateSound method, but resting on assumptions or a limited sample
High model uncertaintyInputs are too thin or too stale for the output to be meaningful

The stale-price problem, measured

This is the single largest data-quality issue for a NEPSE book. A scrip that does not trade carries its last close forward, and the return series reads that as a 0% move. An illiquid holding therefore looks calm when it is really just unpriced, which biases volatility, VaR, beta and correlation downward and flatters both Sharpe and diversification.

Measured on a real 51-name book in this system: 1,014 symbol-days were being treated as 0% returns. Three holdings (KSLY, NICADF, SLK) had not traded at all in 226 sessions, so their computed volatility was 0.0%. KAHL traded 29 of 226 sessions and showed 33.3% annualised volatility against 90.8% when measured only over sessions it actually traded — understated by a factor of 2.7. A separate 10-name liquid book showed no distortion whatsoever.

Holdings are graded Reliable at ≥90% of sessions priced, Estimate between 60% and 90%, and High model uncertainty below 60%. The desk names the affected scrips and states what share of the book they represent, because four unpriced names that are 0.2% of the portfolio matter far less than one that is 20%.

Beta precision

Weekly beta on ~52 observations is not a precise number, and printing it to two decimals implies otherwise. The desk now computes the standard error of each regression. Real examples from these books:

HoldingBetaStd error95% CIRead
UNHPL0.500.24[0.03, 0.97]48% of that book — the true beta could be almost anything
HIMSTAR2.360.53[1.32, 3.40]Directionally high, magnitude uncertain
NIBLGF−0.200.27[−0.72, 0.33]t = −0.73 — indistinguishable from zero
NABIL0.600.065[0.48, 0.73]Genuinely precise

A holding whose |t| < 2 is graded Estimate, and the weight of such holdings drives the grade on the Beta & Stress group. This is the trade-off behind weekly beta: it removes the non-synchronous-trading bias that corrupts daily betas on thin NEPSE names, at the cost of reducing the sample from ~246 observations to ~52.

Assumptions that no amount of data fixes

  • Stress is linear. Impact = β × shock. In a real crash betas rise, correlations converge and liquidity disappears, so losses are typically worse than the scenario shows.
  • Performance is synthetic. Sharpe, Treynor, alpha, M² and the information ratio apply today's weights to past prices. They answer "how would this book have scored", not "what did you earn" — realised performance needs a holdings history, which is not recorded yet.
  • Residuals are assumed uncorrelated in the factor model. Measured on these books, that is false: same-sector residual correlation averaged +0.46 on one and +0.15 on the other, against ≈0 across sectors. Idiosyncratic risk is therefore understated and the factor split flatters diversification.
  • One market factor only. There is no explicit interest-rate, sector, size, value or liquidity factor. It is a single-market-factor decomposition, not a full risk model.
  • Liquidity is modelled, not executable. Days-to-liquidate uses past average volume at an assumed participation rate. Volume tends to vanish exactly when selling is urgent.
  • Historical VaR assumes the past resembles the future. A one-year window may contain no crash at all, in which case VaR understates what a crash would cost. VaR is also a threshold, never a maximum — read Expected Shortfall beside it.
  • Limits are conventions. The 12%/15% single-name thresholds and the rest are monitoring defaults, not derived from your risk appetite or any regulation.

One thing the textbook predicts that this data does not show

Correlations are widely said to converge toward 1 in a crisis, destroying diversification when it is most needed. Split by market direction on these books, the opposite appears: average pairwise correlation in down weeks was 0.253 versus 0.411 in up weeks on one book, and essentially unchanged (0.217 vs 0.224) on the other. The honest reading is not observed in this sample rather than false — the window contains ordinary down weeks, not a genuine crash, and a real crisis may still behave as the textbook says.

Section 5 · Core methodology

Value at Risk — methodology

Purpose. VaR answers: “Over the next N sessions, with X% confidence, what is the most I should expect to lose?” A 1-day 95% VaR of Rs 50,000 means: on 95 sessions out of 100, the loss should not exceed Rs 50,000 — and on ~5 sessions out of 100 it will.

5.1 Historical simulation (primary)

The governing VaR on this desk is historical simulation on the portfolio's own return series, rebuilt with today's weights:

portfolio return r_p,t = Σ wᵢ · rᵢ,t (wᵢ = today's weight; missing print ⇒ rᵢ,t = 0) 10-session return R_p,t = Π(1 + r_p,t−j) − 1, j = 0…9 (overlapping realised windows) VaR₉₅(h) = max(0, − 5th percentile of {R_p,t,h}) VaR₉₉(h) = max(0, − 1st percentile) CVaR / Expected Shortfall = average loss in the tail beyond the matching VaR threshold h = 1 or 10 sessions; both VaR and CVaR are calculated at 95% and 99%
MethodHistorical simulation
Confidence95% and 99%
Lookback370 days (~246 sessions), min 30
Horizons1 day · 10 days (empirical overlapping windows)

Why historical, not normal? NEPSE returns are fat-tailed and circuit-band truncated; a normal-distribution VaR understates tail risk. The parametric figure (1.645 · σ_p for 95%; 2.326 · σ_p for 99%) is shown as a comparison.

5.2 Parametric VaR (per-holding & summary horizons)

The VaR card reports a complete variance-covariance (parametric) comparison matrix for the portfolio: 1-day and 10-day horizons at both 95% and 99% confidence. σ_p is measured from the current-weight portfolio return series, which is algebraically equivalent to applying current weights to the observed covariance matrix and preserves cross-holding diversification.

Parametric VaR₉₅(h) = 1.645 · σ_p,daily · √h Parametric VaR₉₉(h) = 2.326 · σ_p,daily · √h h = 1 or 10 sessions

Per-holding VaR columns and the 1-week / 1-month loss tiles use the parametric (variance-based) form, because a single name's historical percentile is too noisy:

daily σ = annualised vol / √246 VaR₉₅(h sessions) = 1.645 · σ_daily · √h h = 5 (1W) or 20 (1M) ₨ loss = VaR × position value (per holding) · VaR × book value (portfolio, on σ_p)

The portfolio 1W/1M loss tiles use the portfolio's own daily σ_p (correlation between names is already inside the portfolio return series), so they are diversified figures — lower than the sum of the per-holding columns. On thin history they fall back to the undiversified column sum and say so.

Assumptions (all VaR variants)

  • Current weights held constant over the horizon (no rebalancing).
  • √-time scaling applies only to parametric VaR and assumes returns are serially uncorrelated. Historical 10-day VaR/CVaR uses realised compounded windows.
  • A non-trading day counts as a 0% return for the holding (no-move assumption) — reasonable for marking, but it can understate the risk of very thin names.
  • History repeats: the last ~246 sessions are treated as representative of the next one.

7.5 Sensitivity (Beta Forecast) columns

Interpretation. This is the market-driven move only: it assumes the holding responds to the index exactly per its historical weekly beta, with no stock-specific news. Holdings without a beta are held flat (expected price = LTP, G/L = 0), which makes the scenario conservative for those names.

Assumptions. Linear beta response (no convexity), no circuit-band clipping at the holding level, and the move happens over a horizon short enough that beta is stable.

Section 9

Stress Scenarios

book impact % = β_p × market shock % book impact ₨ = impact % × current portfolio value stressed portfolio value = current value + book impact ₨ stress-card NAV = stressed holdings value (broker cash is reported separately and is not shocked) scenarios: current NEPSE level · NEPSE ±5% · ±10% · ±20% · target 3,200 · all-history highest close

Methodology. Deterministic beta propagation: each hypothetical NEPSE move is pushed through the portfolio's weekly-return beta (§4). If beta is unavailable, scenarios are withheld rather than assuming a market beta. Unlike VaR (which asks how likely a loss is), stress asks “if this happens, what does it cost?” — no probability attached.

Outputs. The chart compares gain/loss magnitude, including the zero-shock current market level as the baseline. The table shows target NEPSE level, market move, a single stressed portfolio value/NAV, rupee impact and percentage gain/loss. Broker cash is included in the Overview's Total Equity but intentionally excluded from the market-risk shock; this card is an invested-book view, not accounting NAV per unit.

The all-time-high target comes from the highest NEPSE Index close in local history. When that close is within 1% of 3,200, the two targets are merged into one NEPSE 3,200 / ATH scenario and the exact historical close/date is shown as its reference. This prevents two economically identical scenarios from overstating coverage.

Assumptions. Linear response, instant shock, no rebalancing, no liquidity impact — in a real −20% market, exit costs (§12) come on top. The −20% scenario approximates a severe NEPSE bear leg; compare it with your worst realised session and max drawdown for context.

Section 11

Risk Decomposition & Sector Exposure

Purpose. Splits total portfolio risk into market (systematic) risk — moves with NEPSE, cannot be diversified away — and stock-specific (idiosyncratic) risk — diversifiable by adding uncorrelated names. Then attributes it by sector and by name. The portfolio page combines this risk view with current sector capital weights so the two related measures can be compared without duplicating cards.

single-factor model (weekly): rᵢ = αᵢ + βᵢ·r_m + εᵢ portfolio variance: σ²_p = β_p²·σ²_m + Σ wᵢ²·resid ᵢ² holding risk contribution: RCᵢ = wᵢ·βᵢ·β_p·σ²_m + wᵢ²·residᵢ² (Σ RCᵢ = σ²_p exactly) annualisation: vol = √(variance × 52) × 100 (weekly → yearly)

Inputs. Weekly betas and weekly residual volatilities from the §4 regressions, plus the weekly variance of the NEPSE Index itself — the whole model runs on one consistent (weekly) frequency. Residuals are assumed uncorrelated across names.

Interpretation. A high market share (say 80%) means the book is essentially a leveraged/deleveraged NEPSE tracker — diversifying within NEPSE won't reduce much. A high stock-specific share with a few dominant contributors means single-name risk you can reduce. “Covers X% of book value” shows how much of the portfolio had enough history to model; the remainder sits outside the decomposition rather than distorting it.

Section 12

Liquidity Risk — Days to Liquidate

1-year ADV = total traded quantity over the trailing 365-calendar-day window ÷ market sessions in that window position DTL = kitta ÷ (selected participation rate × ADV) value sold by day D = Σ min(position value, D × participation × ADVᵢ × priceᵢ) portfolio DTL at target X% = first D where value sold by day D ≥ X% × portfolio value
Volume windowTrailing 365 calendar days ≈ 1 trading year
Participation scenarios10% · 20% · 25% of ADV per session
Portfolio targets25% · 50% · 75% · 100% · user-defined
Risk labelsLow ≤1d · Moderate ≤5d · High >5d · Very High no volume
Very HighNo volume in the window

Why ADV divides by market sessions, not trade days: a scrip that printed on 3 of 246 sessions is genuinely thin; averaging over only its trade days would make it look falsely liquid. Participation is a capacity assumption, not a guarantee; lower settings are more conservative.

Portfolio liquidation algorithm. Holdings sell simultaneously up to their own daily capacity. A continuous piecewise-linear solve finds the first day the requested market value is reached. If untradeable value makes a target impossible, the table shows “Not achievable” instead of a fabricated day count.

Card metrics. Sellable in 1/5 days = value-weighted share of the book exitable in that window at the selected participation. Avg days to exit = value-weighted mean DTL over tradeable names. Illiquid positions counts names over 5 days (untradeables shown separately). The milestone table reports 25/50/75/100% and any custom target. Interpretation: stressed-market volume can disappear, so treat DTL as a normal-market capacity estimate, not a guaranteed exit clock.

Section 13

Sector Exposure — Capital Allocation

sector weight = Σ value of holdings in sector ÷ total portfolio value × 100

This is the invested-capital subsection of the combined Risk Decomposition & Sector Exposure card; it is not the factor model's sector risk contribution. Sectors come from the NEPSE company-profile reference table; names without a profile fall into “Uncategorized.” Bars are scaled to the largest sector. Interpretation: NEPSE is banking-heavy, so most local books concentrate in Commercial Banks and Development Banks — the policy check flags any single sector at 30% (watch) / 40% (breach). Remember that sector labels understate linkage: NEPSE financials are highly correlated with each other, so true sector diversification is weaker than the bar chart suggests (see the factor model, §11).

Section 15

Risk-Adjusted Performance

Purpose. Every other card asks how much risk the book carries. This one asks whether that risk was paid for, by comparing the portfolio against the NEPSE Index and against the risk-free rate.

Sharpe = (R_p − R_f) / σ_p total risk Treynor = (R_p − R_f) / β_p systematic risk M² = (R_p − R_f) × (σ_m / σ_p) + R_f re-levered to NEPSE volatility M² alpha = M² − R_m Jensen's alpha = R_p − [R_f + β_p × (R_m − R_f)] vs the CAPM required return tracking error = stdev(r_p − r_m) × √52 annualised information ratio = (R_p − R_m) / tracking error
FrequencyWeekly returns, annualised ×52 / ×√52
Risk-free6% p.a. (≈ 91-day T-bill)
BenchmarkNEPSE Index
Minimum history26 shared weeks

Why weekly. Beta is estimated on weekly returns (§4) because NEPSE names trade thinly and daily betas are biased toward zero. Treynor and Jensen's alpha divide by that beta, so the return and volatility feeding them are sampled weekly too. Mixing a weekly beta with a daily sigma would misstate every ratio on this card.

What each number on the card means

Every figure below is annualised. The worked example in each row uses a real book on the desk today — 52 weeks of history, risk-free 6.00%, β 0.93 — so you can follow the arithmetic straight through.

Portfolio return — −12.52%
What this exact set of holdings, at today's weights, would have returned over the window. It is a geometric (compounded) return, not an average of the weekly numbers, so it answers "what would a rupee have become". Read it next to the benchmark, never alone.

NEPSE return — −7.34%
The same calculation applied to the NEPSE Index over the identical weeks. This is the "doing nothing clever" alternative — what an index tracker would have handed you.

Active return — −5.18%
Portfolio minus benchmark (−12.52 − (−7.34)). The single plainest answer to "did picking these stocks beat just buying the market?". Negative here means the selection cost 5.18 percentage points versus the index. It ignores risk entirely — that is what the ratios below add.

Sharpe — −0.81
Return earned above the risk-free rate per unit of total volatility: (−12.52 − 6.00) ÷ 22.91. It answers "how much reward did each unit of bumpiness buy?". Roughly: above 1.0 is strong, 0.5–1.0 respectable, below 0 means cash in a fixed deposit would have beaten it. Sharpe is only meaningful compared with another portfolio or the index — the number alone has no natural scale.

Treynor — −0.199
The same excess return, but divided by beta instead of volatility: (−0.1252 − 0.06) ÷ 0.93 = −0.199. It prices only the market risk you took, on the assumption the stock-specific risk has been diversified away elsewhere. Use it when this portfolio is one sleeve of a bigger pot; use Sharpe when it is the whole pot.

Mind the units. Sharpe divides a return by a volatility, so it is a pure ratio. Treynor divides a return by beta, so it is still in return units — −0.199 means −19.9 percentage points of excess return per unit of beta, not −0.199%. Sharpe −0.81 and Treynor −0.199 sit side by side on the card but are not on the same scale, so never compare the two figures directly. Compare each against the same measure for another portfolio or for NEPSE.

Jensen's alpha — −6.11%
The most directly interpretable number on the card. CAPM says a book with β 0.93 should have returned R_f + β(R_m − R_f) = 6.00 + 0.93 × (−7.34 − 6.00) = −6.41%. It actually returned −12.52%. The 6.11-point shortfall is alpha. Positive alpha = beat what the risk taken entitled you to; negative = fell short. Unlike Sharpe it stands on its own, and it is the honest ceiling on what active management is worth paying for.

M² alpha — −0.70%
Sharpe restated as a percentage return, which is far easier to feel. It asks: if this portfolio were levered or de-levered until it was exactly as volatile as NEPSE (22.91% → 17.37%), what would it have returned? Answer −8.04%, against NEPSE's −7.34% — so 0.70 points worse on equal footing. Same ranking as Sharpe, expressed in money terms.

Tracking error — 16.29%
The volatility of the difference between the portfolio and the index — how tightly the book shadows NEPSE. Near 0% is an index tracker; 16.29% is a long way from the index, which is expected for a 10-stock concentrated book. High tracking error is not good or bad in itself: it is the size of the bet you are taking away from the benchmark.

Information ratio — −0.32
Active return ÷ tracking error (−5.18 ÷ 16.29). It answers "was the deviation from the index worth it?" — reward per unit of benchmark-relative risk. Above 0.5 is good, above 1.0 rare. Negative means the bets away from NEPSE lost money and added volatility.

Beta (β) — 0.93
Sensitivity to NEPSE: the book moves ~0.93% for each 1% index move. Below 1 is defensive, above 1 is geared. It is the input to Treynor and Jensen's alpha, which is why its estimation frequency matters so much.

Risk-free rate — 6.00%
What you could earn with no risk at all (≈ the 91-day T-bill). Every ratio measures return in excess of this, because return you could have had for free is not a reward for taking risk. It is a fixed assumption in this build, not a live rate.

Reading the example as a whole: the book fell 12.52% while NEPSE fell 7.34%, so it lost more than the market and was more volatile (22.91% vs 17.37%). Every measure agrees — negative alpha, negative M² alpha, negative information ratio. When the measures disagree, that disagreement is informative: it usually means the portfolio is winning on one kind of risk and losing on the other, and which one matters depends on the question in the next paragraph.

Which measure to read. Sharpe and M² use total risk — the right choice when this portfolio is effectively the investor's whole wealth. Treynor and Jensen's alpha use systematic risk — the right choice when it is one sleeve of an already diversified pot. The two can rank the same portfolios differently, and neither ranking is wrong; they answer different questions.

When the ratios invert. Sharpe and Treynor need a positive numerator to mean anything. Below the risk-free rate a riskier portfolio produces a less negative ratio, so the ranking reverses. The card flags this case explicitly and directs you to alpha and active return instead.

Not realised performance. The return series applies current weights to past prices (the same series as portfolio VaR, §5.1). It answers “how would today's book have scored over this window”, not “what did this investor actually earn”. True money-weighted performance needs a stored holdings history, which the desk does not yet keep.

Section 16

Correlation & Diversification Ratio

Purpose. Effective holdings (§14) counts names. This measures whether those names actually move apart. Ten NEPSE banks score well on count and badly here — and the second reading is the one that matters.

ρ(a,b) = Pearson correlation of weekly returns over shared weeks average correlation = mean of all measurable pairs diversification ratio = σ_portfolio / average σ_holding × 100 lower is better
Lowavg ρ < 0.50 — genuinely useful
Moderate0.50 – 0.75
High≥ 0.75 — little benefit left
Minimum overlap26 shared weeks per pair

Interpretation. A diversification ratio of 100% means the portfolio is exactly as volatile as the average holding — combining the names bought nothing. The lower the ratio, the more the holdings offset one another. Both numerator and denominator are computed from the same weekly series so the ratio is not comparing two sampling frequencies.

The point. Adding more holdings does not diversify a portfolio; adding holdings that behave differently does. Pairs are listed most- and least-correlated so it is visible which positions are duplicating an existing exposure and which are doing the real work.

Pairs without 26 shared weeks are skipped rather than estimated, so a recently listed or very thin name may not appear. The average is over measurable pairs only, and is unweighted — it describes the holdings, not the capital behind them.

Section 17 · Appendix

Interpretation guide & limitations

  • Everything is end-of-day. Intraday moves, and anything after the last sync, are invisible to the desk.
  • Total Equity is not fund NAV. It adds signed broker cash to marked holdings, but does not maintain fund units, bank cash, liabilities outside the broker ledger or subscriptions/redemptions. Stress-card NAV remains stressed holdings value.
  • Stale prices flatter risk. A name that hasn't traded for days shows zero return — volatility, VaR and beta for very thin names are best-effort floors, not ceilings. The liquidity card tells you which names to distrust.
  • Circuit bands truncate tails. Realised NEPSE history cannot show a −20% day for a single stock; multi-day losing streaks are how tails express themselves here — watch the 10-day VaR and worst-5-session figures, not just 1-day VaR.
  • Cost-basis coverage is explicit. Current unrealised P/L needs WACC. Realised P/L uses prior ledger buys, falls back to a labelled WACC estimate for a missing opening lot, and remains unresolved when neither source is available.
  • Estimates degrade gracefully. Any metric without enough history shows “—” rather than a fabricated number, and averages re-base over what remains.
  • Not investment advice. The desk is a monitoring tool on your own data.
