Morning Star — How It Works (Simple Language)

Everything the Morning Star tab does, explained in plain words: what each score means, what every parameter (like NII) actually is, and how the stars are given. This is a research screen inspired by the Morningstar style-box idea — not an official Morningstar rating, and not investment advice.

1. The whole thing in short

Every company gets two marks out of 100, always compared only against its own sector:

  • Growth score — is the business getting bigger than its peers? (this year vs last year)
  • Value score — is the share cheap for what you get, compared to peers?

The two marks combine into one number (usually Growth 60% + Value 40%), and that number becomes the star rating (1★–5★). Then the balance sheet acts as a police check: it can never add stars, but a serious problem (bad loans above 4%, weak solvency, heavy debt it can barely service) caps the rating at 2★, and smaller warning signs cut a few points.

Finally each company gets a style tag (Growth / Blend / Value — which mark is leading) and a size tag (Large / Mid / Small — its share of the sector's total market value).

One line: the income statement decides Growth, the market price decides Value, and the balance sheet can only punish — never reward.

2. The idea behind it

Big returns come from earnings growth, not from cheapness alone. Real NEPSE history: Chhimek grew earnings ~21% a year and returned ~63× in 18 years; Standard Chartered, a "good company" with flat earnings, only doubled in the same time. A cheap share whose profit never grows takes 20 years to pay you back — that is what a P/E of 20 with zero growth means.

Every sector has a different engine, so each sector is measured with its own parameters: banks live on loans and interest, life insurers on new policies, hydros on selling electricity, hotels on filling rooms. Using one formula for all of them would compare apples to generators.

3. How a score is made — step by step

  1. Pick the period. The newest quarterly filing for the sector. If fewer than 80% of companies have filed it yet, the previous quarter is used instead (so a few early filers can't distort the ranks).
  2. Compute each parameter from the filed numbers. Growth items compare this year's total-so-far against last year's same total-so-far (never quarter vs quarter — Nepali filings are cumulative).
  3. Turn it into a rank. Each parameter becomes "what % of sector peers does this company beat?" — a number from 0 to 100. Direction matters: a LOWER P/E ranks high, a HIGHER loan growth ranks high.
  4. Average the ranks with weights. Each sector's weights are in section 7. If a number is missing from the filing, that parameter is simply dropped and its weight spreads over the others — missing is never counted as zero. The "n of m factors" tag shows how complete the data was.
  5. Combine. Combined = Growth 60% + Value 40%. Trading, Investment and Mutual Fund use 30% + 70% instead, because those are holdings, not growing businesses.

4. Stars, style, size

Stars

From the combined score (after flag penalties): 80+ → 5★ · 60+ → 4★ · 40+ → 3★ · 20+ → 2★ · below 20 → 1★. Any hard gate (section 5) caps the result at 2★ no matter how high the score.

Style

Growth score minus Value score. Leading by 15+ points → Growth or Value; otherwise Blend. "Value" just means "cheap relative to its momentum" — it is a description, not advice.

Size

Companies are lined up by market value (share price × total kitta — NEPSE's own capitalisation figure where the filing has no share count; fund size for mutual funds; face value never matters). Walk down the line adding each company's value to a running total: names reached before 70% of the sector's pot are Large, between 70–90% are Mid, and the last 10% are Small.

5. Quality gates & flags — the police check

Hard gates (rating capped at 2★)

  • Negative EPS — the company is losing money.
  • Book value per share below par — accumulated losses have eaten into the capital shareholders paid in (Rs 100 par; SHL Rs 10, HATHY Rs 50). A low P/B on a wrecked balance sheet is not "value", and growth off a near-zero base is not growth. Negative book value is the same gate, worse.
  • NPL above 4% — more than 4 rupees of every 100 lent has gone bad (lenders).
  • Capital below the regulator's floor — no cushion left to absorb losses (lenders).
  • Solvency below 1.0 — an insurer that might not cover all claims if things go wrong.
  • Interest coverage below 1.5× — a hydro barely earning enough to pay its loan interest.
  • One-time income above 30% of profit — the "profit" mostly came from selling assets or paper gains, not the business. Next year it disappears.

A hard gate does two things: it caps the rating at 2★, and it bars the company from the High Growth and High Value halves of the quadrant map however high its scores. The scores are still shown — the gate is the reason they cannot be trusted.

Soft flags (−5 points each, max −15)

  • Inventory growing much faster than sales — unsold goods piling up; discounting comes next.
  • Receivables growing much faster than sales — sales made to people who haven't paid yet.
  • Provision coverage falling / NPL rising sharply — a lender's safety cushion thinning.
  • Current ratio below 1 — bills due soon exceed the money available to pay them.
  • Fair-value gains above 30% of profit — an insurer's profit came from marking up investments.

6. Every parameter explained in one line

Banking / Finance / Microfinance

NII (Net Interest Income)Interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits — a bank's core profit engine. If NII shrinks, the bank is shrinking.
Loan growthHow much bigger the loan book got. Loans today = interest income tomorrow. Only good if the loans stay healthy.
Deposit growthDeposits are a bank's cheap raw material. No deposit growth → must borrow expensively → thinner profit.
Fee incomeCommissions, cards, remittance, guarantees — money earned without lending, so no credit risk. The highest-quality income a bank has.
Distributable profitThe profit legally allowed to become your dividend, after forced reserve transfers. EPS can be 16 while distributable is only 12 — we score the one you can actually receive.
P/B vs ROEPrice-to-book alone is meaningless: a bank earning 20% on equity deserves a high P/B, one earning 5% doesn't. We score cheapness relative to quality.
NIM / Net interest spreadThe gap between the lending rate and deposit rate — profit per rupee lent. High spread = pricing power.
NPL (Non-Performing Loan)Share of loans not being repaid. 1% bad loans on a huge book can erase a whole year's profit — this is what kills banks.
Provision coverageMoney already set aside against bad loans, vs the bad loans recognized. High = the pain is already absorbed.
CAR / Capital fundThe regulator's minimum capital cushion. Near the floor = no room to grow; below it = gate.
Cost of funds (microfinance)What it pays to borrow the money it lends out. When Nepal's rates fall, this falls, and the saving goes straight to profit.
Accumulated distributable PS (microfinance)Years of undistributed profit piled up per share — a stored future dividend you buy with the share.

Insurance

First-year premium (life)Money from brand-new policies sold this year — the purest sign the sales engine works. Each new policy pays for 10–20 more years.
Renewal premium (life)Old policies still being paid. Falling renewals = customers cancelling.
Policies in force (life)Raw count of active policies. Cross-check: premium up but policies down = fewer customers buying bigger tickets.
Life fundThe big pool of policyholder money the insurer invests. Bigger fund = bigger future investment income.
MCap / gross premium (life)Company price vs premium collected. Used instead of P/E because insurer "profit" is an actuarial estimate, while premium is real cash.
Bonus rate (life)The bonus declared to policyholders — the closest thing to a life insurer's dividend track record. A cut here is a red flag before EPS shows it.
Investment income / yieldInterest and dividends earned on the fund. Insurers park most premium in FDs — when FD rates crash, profit crashes with nothing wrong in the insurance itself.
Combined ratio (non-life)(Claims + expenses) ÷ premium earned. Below 100% = the insurance itself makes money; above = it survives only on investment income. The single most important non-life number.
Claim ratio vs own historyClaims ÷ premium, compared to the company's own past. One flood year looks terrible vs peers but normal vs history — so we compare to itself.
Retention ratioShare of premium the company keeps vs passes to reinsurers. Keeping more of PROFITABLE risk = more earning power.
SolvencyRegulator's "can it pay all claims in a disaster" ratio. Below 1.0 = gate.

Hydropower

Core revenueMoney from actually selling electricity — nothing else. Hydro headline profits are routinely inflated by one-off share-market gains, so we strip them.
Core profit / core P/EProfit (and the P/E built on it) after removing forex gains, dividend income and other side income. One 474M paper gain was 43% of a real company's PBT — next year it vanished.
Finance-cost improvementIs the interest bill falling? Hydros are ~75% debt — as loans repay or rates drop, interest saved becomes pure profit (the "J-curve" climb).
CWIP growthCapital Work In Progress — plants under construction. Growing CWIP = a new plant (a revenue step-jump) approaching.
Interest coverageOperating profit ÷ interest bill. Below 1.5× means one dry year from being unable to service the loan — gate.
D/E (Debt/Equity)How much of the plant is financed by loans vs owners' money. Lower = more of the revenue belongs to shareholders.

Manufacturing, Hotels, Trading, Investment, Funds

Gross margin trend (mfg)Profit per rupee of sales, direction over the year. Rising = pricing power; revenue up but margin collapsing = buying sales with discounts.
ROCE (mfg)Operating profit ÷ all money used (equity + debt). "Is the factory a good machine for turning capital into profit?" — the note's ROIC test.
Inventory vs revenue (flag)Stock growing 27% while sales fall 11% = warehouses filling with unsold goods.
Operating leverage (hotels)Profit growth minus revenue growth. Hotels have fixed costs, so past break-even every extra guest is nearly pure profit — profit growing 2–3× faster than revenue is the sector's most bullish sign.
EV / operating profit (hotels)What you pay (price + debt − cash) for the cash the hotel operation throws off, ignoring depreciation on buildings that actually appreciate.
Net current assets / MCap (trading)Graham's test: if cash + stock + receivables minus ALL debts is worth more than the whole company's price, you're buying the business for less than nothing.
Reserves / paid-up (trading)Decades of kept profit vs original capital (15.8× at one company) — a loaded spring for bonus shares.
NAV discount (investment / funds)The share trades below the per-share value of what it holds. A 15% discount = paying Rs 85 for Rs 100 of assets.
NAV total return (funds)Did the manager grow the money? The entire product, in one number.

8. Reading the charts

Growth vs Value maps (one per size tier): each dot is one company — right = growing faster than peers, up = cheaper than peers. The dividers sit at the sector median of each score, so the tinted top-right corner is exactly the High Growth / High Value quadrant in the table. Amber dots = 4★/5★. Hollow red rings = quality-gated: plotted where they scored, but not eligible for the sweet spot whatever their position. Blue = everything else. Hover a dot for exact numbers; the "show quality-gated" box hides the rings when you only want the eligible set.

A red ring deep in the sweet spot is the most important pattern on the page: the market is pricing it cheap-and-growing because of the balance-sheet problem — for a hydro, usually a book value below par. Decide whether the problem is temporary before being tempted.

9. Warnings

  • Ranks are relative. A 90 Growth score in a shrinking sector means "least bad", not "growing". Open the row and read the raw numbers.
  • Early filing season: some sectors score on the previous quarter until enough companies file (the period label always says which quarter was used).
  • Amber confidence tag = many factors missing; the score stands on fewer legs.
  • Not in the score: hydro real generation vs design (DOED portal), PPA terms, transmission access, subsidiary holdings, remaining tax-holiday years, fund expense ratios. Check these by hand.
  • This is a screen for further research — not a buy/sell signal and not investment advice.
