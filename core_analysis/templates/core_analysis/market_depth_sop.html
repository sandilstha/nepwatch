Market Depth — how it works

The broker's trading system shows, for every share, the five best buy prices and the five best sell prices, with how many shares are waiting at each and how many separate orders make up that quantity. This tab stores those snapshots through the day and lets you replay them. It answers one question: what changed in the order book, how big was it for this share, and how fast did it happen? It does not judge anyone. This page explains every number, and what the numbers cannot tell you.

1. What this tab does

Every other tab on this desk reads trades that already happened. This one reads the orders that were waiting to trade, before and after each moment of the day.

A large order sitting in the book is visible to every trader. When it appears, other traders may react. When it leaves, the picture changes again. The tab makes those appearances and departures visible after the fact, measured against what is normal for that particular share, so that 5,000 shares reads as huge for a thin stock and ordinary for a liquid one.

Example from 9 September 2026, SHL. At 11:23 a bid of 103,500 shares appeared at 495, about 65 times the depth the share normally shows. It started as one order and grew to five. At 11:30 all of it was gone and the bid side had fallen from 105,200 shares to 1,900. Mid price went from 503 to 500. Nothing on this page says why. It says exactly what, in numbers you can check.
This is the first stage of a longer design: raw observations only. There is no score, no verdict and no name attached to any order. Section 12 says what will be added and in what order.

2. Where the data comes from

A collector on the feed host polls the broker trading system (TMS) during market hours and stores one capture per share per visit. The platform syncs those captures into its own table with manage.py sync_market_depth, keyed on the feed's row id so a day can be re-synced without duplicates. History on the feed starts 5 August 2026.

FieldMeaning
Capture timeWall-clock time the book was read for that share
Bid levels 1 to 5Best five buy prices, each with quantity and order count
Ask levels 1 to 5Best five sell prices, each with quantity and order count
Total bids / asksQuantity across the whole book, not only the top five
The collector visits shares in rotation, not all at once. On a typical day a liquid share is captured every 26 seconds at the median, but the gap stretches to two minutes one time in ten and to several minutes at worst. Thin shares may be captured only every ten minutes. Everything on this tab is limited by that gap. See section 11.

3. What a frame is

A frame is one capture of one share. Frames for a share are placed in time order, and every measurement compares a frame with the previous frame of the same share. There is no market-wide snapshot; two shares captured in the same second are a coincidence, not a batch.

The slider on the replay steps through frames. The frame label shows the share, the capture time, the frame number, and the seconds since the previous capture. That last number matters: a change that took 26 seconds and a change that took 400 seconds are not the same event, even if the shares moved are identical.

4. Reading the book

The bid table and the ask table each show the five levels of the current frame.

ColumnWhat it is
Lvl1 is the best price on that side. Level numbers are for reading only; comparisons use the price.
PriceThe price of that level
QtyShares waiting at that price, all orders combined
SplitsHow many separate orders make up that quantity
Qty/splitAverage shares per order at that price
Δ qtyChange in quantity at that price since the previous frame. Green grew, red shrank, new means the price was not in the top five before.

The header of each table shows the top-5 total, the whole-book total, and the number of orders. Comparing top-5 with the whole book tells you whether the visible depth is most of what exists or only the front of a much deeper queue.

Why price, not level number. If the best bid is filled, every other bid moves up one level without anyone touching it. A comparison by level would report a phantom pull at one level and a phantom add at the next. The tab therefore matches levels by price across frames. An order that slides from level 4 to level 3 is reported as unchanged.

5. Baseline and spike

The tab never uses a fixed share count as a threshold. Instead each share carries its own baseline: the median of its top-5 quantity over the previous 20 frames, computed separately for the bid side and the ask side.

Spike is the current top-5 quantity divided by that baseline. A spike of 3× means three times this share's own recent normal. The same 3× on a thin share and on a liquid share means the same thing.

RuleValueWhy
Baseline window20 framesLong enough to smooth, short enough to follow the session. Not yet tuned against labelled cases.
Minimum history5 framesNo baseline, so no spike and no event, until the share has been seen five times that day
Minimum baseline100 sharesA book that normally holds five shares turns every order into a thousand-fold "spike". Below this the baseline is treated as unknown.
Early in the session every share has a short history, so spikes in the first few frames deserve less weight than the same number at noon. The minimum-history rule removes the worst of this but not all of it.

6. Add and pull events

Between two frames the tab finds, on each side, the single price whose quantity changed the most. If that change is at least half of the side's baseline and at least 100 shares, the frame gets an event label.

LabelMeaning
ADDQuantity appeared at that price, or a price entered the top five with size
PULLQuantity left that price, or the price dropped out of the top five
noneNothing on either side changed by half the baseline

When both sides qualify, the larger one against its baseline is shown. Each event carries:

  • Shares moved, and % of level, where -100% means the price emptied and new means it had nothing before.
  • vs baseline: shares moved divided by the side's baseline. This is the size that matters.
  • Splits before and after, so you can see whether one order or many made the move.
  • Gap in seconds since the previous frame, and shares per second, which is only a rough speed because the move could have happened anywhere inside that gap.
  • Mid after: the mid price in this frame, for a first look at whether price responded.
A pull is not a cancel. Quantity leaving a price can mean the order was withdrawn, or it traded. Only the floorsheet settles which, and matching the two is a later stage. Until then, read PULL as "the shares stopped being displayed", nothing more.

7. Orders, not just shares

The order count at each price is the most useful single field on this tab. Ten thousand shares in one order and ten thousand shares in thirty-five orders look identical in quantity and behave completely differently.

  • One or two orders holding most of a level can leave in a single frame. Many orders drain gradually.
  • An ADD that arrives with one order, then gains orders at the same price, then loses all of them at once, is the shape worth studying. The SHL example in section 1 went 1 → 5 → 0.
  • Qty/split on the frame observations gives the same reading for the whole side in one number.
An order count is order-book structure. It does not say that one order is one person, and the tab never treats it that way.

8. Price on this tab

The feed does not carry the last traded price. The tab uses the mid of the best bid and best ask instead, and shows the spread as a percentage of mid. When one side is empty the mid is simply the other side's best price. Treat it as a proxy: it moves when the book moves, not when a trade prints.

10. How to use it

  1. Open the overview for the day and look at the top ten rows. Ignore any share whose gap is over about two minutes unless the event is very large.
  2. Replay each candidate. Step to the ADD frame and note: one order or many, what fraction of the whole book, what the other side looked like.
  3. Step forward. Did the price persist? Did other bids or asks build around it? Did the mid move?
  4. Step to the PULL frame. Note the order count going to zero or not, the gap, and the mid after.
  5. Open the Script Deep Dive or Broker Flow Map for the same share and window. Did shares trade at that price around that time? That is what separates a fill from a withdrawal.
  6. Write down what you saw and whether you would call it ordinary, unusual, or unclear. Those notes are the labelled cases that any future score will be tested against.
The tab is most useful the day after an unusual move, replayed slowly, with the floorsheet open beside it.

11. What it cannot see

  • Anything faster than the capture gap. An order that appears and leaves between two captures never existed as far as the tab knows. Pull speed is measured in tens of seconds at best.
  • Who placed an order. The feed has no broker or client on it, and the tab never guesses.
  • Why an order left. Cancel and fill look identical here until floorsheet matching is built.
  • The last traded price. Mid is a stand-in.
  • Depth beyond the top five, except as a total.
  • Market context. The NEPSE index is on a sibling feed and is not joined yet.
  • Whether other traders responded. Bids often rise after a large order simply because the market rose. A fair test needs a control rate, and that has not been built.
  • Coverage. Roughly 280 shares are captured, not every listed share, and thin ones only occasionally.

13. Observation, inference, conclusion

The tab is built to keep these three apart, and readers should too.

Observation: 103,500 shares appeared on the bid at 495, 65 times baseline, in one order.
Observation: the order count at that price rose to five over the next frames.
Observation: all of it left within one capture gap of 414 seconds; the mid moved from 503 to 500.
Inference: a large displayed bid that did not stay. Whether it traded is not known from the book.
Conclusion: none yet. That requires the floorsheet, the index, and more cases like it.

Nothing on this desk states that a broker or a person did anything. It states what the order book showed, and how unusual that was for the share.
