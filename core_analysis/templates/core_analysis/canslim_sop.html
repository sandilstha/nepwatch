CAN SLIM — how this screen works

Written in plain language. Every rule the screen uses is on this page.

1. What is CAN SLIM?

CAN SLIM is a way of looking for strong growth stocks, made famous by the American investor William O'Neil. Each letter is one question about a stock:

LetterQuestionOn this screen
CDid the latest quarter's profit grow strongly?Measured
AHas profit grown year after year?Measured
NIs something new happening — a new high, new product?Partly measured
SIs the price rising with strong trading volume?Measured
LIs it a leader — beating the rest of the market?Measured
IAre big funds buying it?Not available
MIs the whole market going up?Shown as a banner

2. Where each number comes from

  • C — Current earnings. We take the company's latest reported quarter and compare its profit with the same quarter one year ago. Same quarter, because Nepali company profits are seasonal — comparing Q2 with Q1 would mistake the season for growth. More than +25% earns a high score.
  • A — Annual earnings. We look at up to five full years of profit and ask two things: how fast did it grow on average, and how often did it grow? A company that grew in 3 of 4 years beats one whose average is carried by a single huge rebound year. The screen shows this as, for example, (3/4).
  • N — New high. How close is today's price to the highest price of the last 52 weeks? At or near the high scores well. Note: O'Neil's "N" also means new products or new management — that information is not in our data, so our N covers the price part only, and says so.
  • S — Supply and demand. Is recent trading volume (last 25 sessions) bigger than normal (last 100 sessions), and did the price rise on that volume? Heavy trading while the price falls does not count — that is selling, not demand.
  • L — Leader or laggard. The stock's price change over roughly 6 months, minus NEPSE's change over the same period. Beating the market by a wide margin makes a leader.
  • M — Market direction. Is the NEPSE index above or below its own 50-session and 150-session averages? This is the same for every stock, so it is shown once, as a banner above the table — not mixed into each stock's score. O'Neil's advice: in a downtrend, be very careful no matter how good a stock looks.

3. How the score out of 100 is built

  • Each measurable factor (C, A, N, S, L) gets 0–10 points from fixed rules — for example C: over +100% = 10, over +25% = 8, over 0% = 4.
  • The total is shown out of 100: points earned ÷ points possible.
  • If a factor cannot be measured, it is left out of the calculation — the stock is not punished for data that does not exist. But if fewer than 3 of the 5 factors can be measured, the stock gets no score at all (usually a new listing).
  • Rows where only 3 or 4 factors were measurable carry a small thin / partial tag so you can see the score rests on less.

4. Honest labels you will see, and why

  • "Recovery" — the company went from a loss last year to a profit now. That is good news, but it is not a growth percentage: the maths of "percent growth from a loss" gives a nonsense negative number, so we never print one.
  • "N/A — loss base" — the company was loss-making in both periods. No percentage is possible. It still receives a low C score (1/10), because two losing quarters in a row is the worst C outcome, not a missing one.
  • "thin history" — fewer than 3 measured years of profit growth, so the A factor is not scored. One spectacular year should not pass for a track record.
  • "±" next to a ticker — a bonus or rights share adjustment happened inside the price window. On that day the price drops sharply for a reason that is not real selling. We skip that day when computing the price factors, so N, S and L for this stock are approximate.

5. What is missing, said plainly

I — Institutional Sponsorship — cannot be measured in Nepal. In the US, funds must publish what they own; NEPSE has no such disclosure. We considered using broker floorsheet flows as a substitute and decided against it: we tested that signal ourselves and it did not predict returns, so dressing it up as "institutional buying" would be misleading. The screen scores 5 factors and says so, rather than faking a sixth.

6. The most important warning

This is a screen, not a prediction. The scoring rules are O'Neil's published rules; they have not been tested against NEPSE's history, and a high score is not a promise the stock will rise. Use the screen to build a shortlist worth researching — the research is still yours to do.

7. Data quirks we handle for you

Nepali company filings are messy. Three problems are corrected before anything is scored:

  • Quarterly numbers are running totals. A company's "Q4 profit" as filed is actually the whole year. We subtract each quarter from the one before to get the real three-month figure — otherwise Q4 would look about 3× too big.
  • The same fact is filed under different names ("Net Income", "NET PROFIT", two different spellings of "EPS (Annualized)"), and for some companies one of those versions is junk — literal zeros or placeholder digits. For each company we pick the one series whose numbers look like real accounts, and use only it. We never mix two sources in one calculation: mixing once produced a fake "+112,254% growth".
  • Fiscal years are Nepali. "2025/26 Q4" is the fourth quarter of the Nepali fiscal year, compared against "2024/25 Q4" — never against a different quarter.
Technical appendix — exact thresholds (for checking our work)
  • C ladder (discrete quarter YoY %): ≥100→10, ≥50→9, ≥25→8, ≥10→6, ≥0→4, ≥−25→2, below→0. Recovery→6. Still loss-making→1.
  • A ladder (avg annual growth %): ≥50→10, ≥25→8, ≥10→6, ≥0→4; then multiplied by 0.4 + 0.6 × (positive years ÷ measured years). Needs ≥3 measured year-transitions and ≥2 full years on file.
  • N (position in 52-week range %): ≥95→10, ≥85→8, ≥70→6, ≥50→4, ≥25→2. Range clamped to 0–100; a close above the stored high counts as a new high.
  • S (25-session avg volume ÷ 100-session): ≥2.0→10, ≥1.5→8, ≥1.2→7, ≥1.0→5, below→0; minus 4 if price did not rise over the same 25 sessions.
  • L (126-session return minus index return, pp): ≥50→10, ≥25→9, ≥10→7, ≥0→5, ≥−25→3, below→0.
  • M: index vs its 50/150-session simple averages; uptrend needs price > rising 50-avg > 150-avg.
  • Ex-date splice: any one-day raw return below −12% (NEPSE's circuit is 10%) is treated as a corporate action and contributes 0 to the compounded return; the row is flagged ±.
  • Universe: ordinary equities traded in the last 60 days, minus debentures, promoter shares, preference shares and funds (same exclusion list as the A/D Radar). Scan cached 15 minutes.
