A/D Radar — how it works

NEPSE publishes a record of every trade, and that record names the buying broker and the selling broker. This tab reads those records and answers one question for each company: is someone quietly building up a position, or quietly getting out? This page explains, in plain words, how it works, how to read it, and — importantly — what it cannot tell you.

1. What this tab does

Every other tab on this desk starts with a broker. This one starts with a company.

On the other tabs you pick a broker and see what it traded. Here you look at the whole market and ask, for each company: who has been buying it, and who has been selling it?

The idea behind “accumulation” is simple. If someone wants to build a large position, they cannot buy it all at once — that would push the price up and cost them more. So they buy steadily, over weeks, often through several different brokers. If you can see the broker records, you can sometimes see that happening. “Distribution” is the same thing in reverse: someone steadily getting out.

A simple picture. Imagine 60 people sell shares of a company over a month, and just 3 buyers end up holding almost all of those shares. That is very different from 60 people selling and 60 people buying. The amount traded is the same — but in the first case somebody is collecting, and in the second nothing in particular is happening.

Two things about this are built in deliberately:

  • It looks at many days, not one. Building a position takes weeks, so a single day tells you nothing. The shortest window offered is one month.
  • It looks at several brokers, not just the biggest one. Buying is often spread across a few brokers, so the tab shows you how many were involved.
Everything here describes trades that have already happened. It is a record of what took place, not a prediction of what comes next. Section 6 explains why that distinction matters more than you might expect.

2. The three things it measures

For each company, the tab works out every broker's net position for the period — how much it bought minus how much it sold. A broker that bought 10,000 shares and sold 2,000 has a net of +8,000. Then it measures three things.

a) How much ended up with the big buyers — “Absorbed”

Take the five brokers who ended up holding the most, add up what they gained, and compare it to everything traded in that period.

If a company traded 100,000 shares during the month and the top five net buyers came away holding 40,000 more than they started with, Absorbed is 40%. A high number means a lot of what changed hands ended up concentrated in a few pairs of hands.

b) Was it one broker, or several — “Top Broker” and “Buy Group”

Top Broker is the share of all the net buying done by the single largest buyer. Buy Group is how many brokers it took to account for half of the net buying.

Buy Group 1 and Top Broker 90% means one broker did nearly everything. Buy Group 5 and Top Broker 25% means the buying was spread across several.

c) Was the selling concentrated — “Sell Conc.”

This measures whether the shares came from one seller or many. It runs from near 0 (hundreds of small sellers) to 10,000 (a single seller providing everything).

Low Sell Conc. + high Absorbed = many people sold, a few people collected. That is the accumulation pattern.
High Sell Conc. = one party is releasing a lot of stock into many small buyers. That is the distribution pattern.

Putting them together

Each company is compared against every other company in the market that day — not against a fixed target. So a score of “high” means “more of this than most other shares right now”, which keeps it meaningful whether the market is busy or quiet.

Technical detail

Per broker, net = shares bought − shares sold over the window. Across all brokers that sums to exactly zero, so neither ratio divides by it. sum_pos is the total of the positive nets; sum_neg is the total of the negative nets in absolute terms; the two are equal by construction and this is verified on every observation. volume is gross shares traded (the sum of every broker's buy quantity), which includes a broker's self-crosses.

Shown asNameExactly
Absorbedabsorb(sum of the top-5 positive nets) ÷ volume — a net numerator over a gross denominator
Top Brokertop1_share(largest single positive net) ÷ sum_pos
Sell Conc.sell_hhiHerfindahl index of the negative nets as shares of sum_neg, ×10,000

Each is converted to a z-score across all scored companies for that date, then combined with equal weights: score = z(absorb) − z(top1_share) − z(sell_hhi). Equal weights are a deliberate choice — fitted weights would be roughly (+1.00, −0.87, −0.41) and score better on the same data they were fitted to, which is exactly the trap described in section 6.

A worked example — NADEP, one-month window

An ordinary Accumulation reading, followed all the way through. Nadep Laghubittiya had 38 different sellers and 23 buyers, with no single broker dominating either side. All three ingredients lean the same way, none of them dramatically — which is what a typical reading looks like.

IngredientRawz-scoreSignContributes
absorb33.2%+0.97++0.97
top1_share18.0%−0.30−+0.30
sell_hhi668−0.55−+0.55
score+1.81
Reading it in words. The top five buyers ended up with 33% of everything traded, which is more than most companies that month — so absorb helps. The largest single buyer took only 18% of the net buying, so the buying was spread rather than one broker's doing — top1_share is below average, and because the formula subtracts it, that helps too. And the selling came from many hands (sell_hhi 668 against a market median near 957), so sell_hhi helps as well. Three modest pushes in the same direction give +1.81, near the top of the market that month: Accumulation.

The other end works identically. Everest Colour scored −1.95 the same month from absorb 9.6% (z −1.04, contributing −1.04), top1_share 21.7% (z −0.01, +0.01) and sell_hhi 2,427 (z +0.91, −0.91): little was absorbed, and what selling there was came from fewer hands — Distribution.

Both examples are snapshots of one rolling one-month window and will change as the window moves. The arithmetic is what matters, not these particular figures.

3. Reading the table

Companies are sorted by score and split into five equal groups — top fifth, next fifth, and so on.

ReadingMeans
AccumulationTop 20%. Heavy buying, spread across brokers, coming from many different sellers.
Mild AccumulationLeaning that way, but weaker.
NeutralMiddle 20%. Buying and selling look ordinary — nobody is dominating.
Mild DistributionLeaning toward the selling side.
DistributionBottom 20%. Concentrated selling into scattered buyers.

Neutral is a real answer, not a missing one. It means nobody is controlling that share — which is genuinely worth knowing, and is the case for most companies most of the time.

4. The four lists

  • Accumulation candidates — the top fifth.
  • Quiet Accumulation candidates — the top fifth and the price did not rise. Someone took a lot of stock without paying up for it. Usually the most interesting list.
  • Distribution candidates — the bottom fifth.
  • All Scored — every company, so you can find a specific one.
The word candidates is used on purpose. Heavy buying with a flat price looks like quiet accumulation, but it is equally consistent with ordinary two-sided trading in a dull market. The list tells you where to look, not what is true.

5. Which companies are included

Only ordinary shares that trade enough to measure.

RuleWhy
Must trade at least Rs 5 lakh a dayA share that barely trades has no pattern to read.
No debenturesThey are loans, not shares. “Accumulation” means nothing for them, and they trade through one or two brokers, which distorts everything.
No mutual fundsFund units behave differently from company shares.
No promoter sharesThey are locked in and cannot be freely traded, so their movement is not market activity.

The count of everything excluded is printed under the table on every scan — a screen that quietly drops half the market would look like “nothing is happening” when it really means “we did not look”.

Why a rupee amount rather than a share count. An earlier version required 800 shares a day. But 800 shares of a Rs 200 share is Rs 1.6 lakh, while 800 shares of a Rs 5,300 share is Rs 42 lakh — the same rule was 26 times stricter on expensive shares. A rupee amount treats every company the same way.

The sector dropdown only narrows the list you see. Every company is still scored against the whole market, so a Trading-sector share with just one peer is still ranked against all ~270 companies rather than against its single neighbour.

6. Does it actually work?

Honest answer: as a description of what happened, yes. As a prediction of what happens next — no, we could not show that.

What we did

We took 3½ years of trade records (2023 to 2026), scored every company every month, and then checked what those shares did afterwards. To be fair about it we:

  • only used information available at the time — no peeking ahead;
  • adjusted for bonus shares, otherwise a 14% bonus looks like a 14% crash;
  • compared each share against the market that day, so a rising market cannot make a useless signal look clever.

The result that matters

At first it looked excellent. But that test had a flaw: we had chosen what to measure using the same years we then tested on. That is like picking the winning horse after watching the race.

So we did it properly — decided everything using only 2023–2024, then tested on 2025–2026, which the model had never seen:

About 85% of the apparent advantage disappeared once the model faced data it had not been built on. Testing year by year tells the same story: 2024 looked strong, 2025 and 2026 showed nothing.

This is why the tab carries a banner saying it is not a validated predictive signal. An earlier version of this page showed the good-looking numbers as if they proved something. They did not, and leaving them up would have been misleading.

Why it faded

The “Absorbed” measure was doing nearly all the work, and it simply stopped lining up with future prices after 2024. Markets change; a pattern that worked in one period need not work in the next. That is normal, and it is exactly why holding back some data to test on matters.

Technical detail

Out-of-sample, forward-disjoint sampling (anchors spaced at least one full horizon apart per symbol, so forward returns never overlap), standard errors clustered by anchor date.

HorizonnSpreadtp
20 sessions4,525+0.80%2.050.040
60 sessions1,500+1.74%1.510.131

Walk-forward (train on all prior years, test on the next): 2024 +13.16% (t=2.27, p=0.024); 2025 +1.05% (t=0.38); 2026 +2.78% (t=0.94).

Feature stability, univariate correlation with 120-session forward excess return: absorb +0.187 (t=5.73) in 2023–24 falling to +0.026 (t=0.70) in 2025–26; top1_share +0.018 then −0.004 — no univariate power in either period, so the earlier claim that dispersed buying beats concentrated buying is retracted; sell_hhi −0.051 then −0.029, weak but sign-consistent.

Earlier figures reported anchors spaced by the 25-session formation window and described as "non-overlapping". That was wrong for longer horizons — consecutive 120-session forward returns shared 95 of their sessions — and overstated significance.

Implementation parity: the research pipeline and the shipped code were compared company-by-company on three historical windows. Maximum difference 0.05 percentage points (display rounding). What runs is what was tested.

7. What we tried that failed

Recorded because a tool is only trustworthy if its failures are visible too.

IdeaWhat happened
“The buyers kept buying day after day” — counting consecutive days of buyingDid not predict anything. What mattered was how much was absorbed, not over how many separate days.
Volume going up versus the previous periodAdded nothing once absorption was known.
Number of brokers in the buying group on its ownNot useful by itself.

The classic “price and volume” rule also failed here

The textbook rule says: high volume with a flat or rising price = accumulation. We tested that on the same data. Shares matching that description went on to do worse than the market average (+1.48% against +2.96%). The volume part contributed nothing at all.

Our reading is that price-and-volume was always an indirect guess at who was buying — useful before broker-level records existed. NEPSE publishes the broker on every trade, so this tab measures the thing itself instead of guessing at it.

8. How to use it

  • Pick a period. One month is the default. Three months gives a steadier picture. A company that shows up in both is a more convincing case than one appearing in only the shorter window.
  • Start with Quiet Accumulation. Buying without the price rising is the most interesting pattern, because the market has not reacted yet.
  • Open the row. Look at the actual brokers on each side.
  • Check the company elsewhere. Use Script Deep Dive for the broker split and Stock 360 for fundamentals. This tab is one input among several, never the whole decision.
  • Do not act on the score by itself. Section 6 explains why.

9. What it cannot see

Shares can move without appearing here at all. Transfers made directly between demat accounts, shares pledged to a bank, and privately negotiated deals never appear in the trade records. Someone can build a large holding entirely this way and this tab will show nothing. A Neutral reading means “no on-market pattern detected” — it does not mean “nothing is happening”.
  • It cannot tell you why anyone traded. The records name the broker, not the client or the reason. A broker with many ordinary retail customers can look exactly like one large deliberate buyer. Everything here describes the shape of trading, never anyone's intention.
  • Concentration is not proof of anything improper. Plenty of innocent explanations produce the same pattern.
  • A high score is not a buy signal. Section 6 covers this in full.
  • Heavily self-traded shares score lower than they should. When one broker is on both sides of a trade, it inflates the total traded figure without changing anyone's net position, which pushes the “Absorbed” percentage down. We left this as-is because the testing was done this way, and changing it would mean shipping something untested.
  • Scores compare companies within one scan only — not across different dates or different periods.
  • Not investment advice. It shows you where to look.
